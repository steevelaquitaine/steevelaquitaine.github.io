Teaching

Teaching statement

To me, teaching means helping students think critically within a discipline: learn its knowledge, recognise its central problems and design practical ways to solve them. I have taught the scientific method to biology undergraduates on site, and machine-learning engineering to professional data scientists, on site and online during the COVID-19 pandemic. These audiences called for different methods and materials, but one principle guided both: students stay focused and motivated when they learn actively, by working together on a project in small groups.

Teaching undergraduates to reason scientifically

For two years, I was a teaching assistant in Scientific Methodology for first- and second-year biology students at the University of Bordeaux, in a class of 20. The goal was to teach them to reason scientifically. Lectures and readings gave them the basic biology this required, but we made active learning the priority: each week, students worked on a research project on a topic they had chosen. I formed groups of four, and each week I supervised them at the library while they negotiated their topic, judged the relevance of articles and divided the work.

The syllabus set the same expectations for everyone, but differences in maturity make shared goals harder to reach for the weakest students. We therefore used a questionnaire to group students with similar interests and maturity. This let us steer the weaker groups toward easier topics and freed time to explain difficult concepts.

The course built up through four intermediate outcomes to two capstones:

  1. Notes. An individual first assignment, to make students own their learning: take, review and organise notes during a seminar I gave on my PhD research, and hand them in.
  2. Article analysis. In class, each group chose research articles, broke down their logic and argued for their importance.
  3. Abstract. Building on that analysis, students summarised an article in a written abstract.
  4. Lab report. They performed a biochemistry experiment and described it in a written report.
  5. Literature review (capstone). Each group wrote a literature review.
  6. Oral defence (capstone). Groups presented their review with slides and answered questions.

Choosing their own topic motivated students to engage in class, and I gave them opportunities to explain their research to other groups, as experts, in their own words. Throughout the course, I prompted reflection and asked questions, which quickly revealed misconceptions and roadblocks, and I rewarded participation with positive feedback and extra credit. The highlight of the course was a visit to my laboratory, where students watched me perform an experiment with monkeys. It made research concrete for them.

Teaching machine-learning engineering to professionals

As lead instructor in the banking sector, I taught three teams of data scientists the practical skills of machine-learning engineering. The central goal was for them to learn to develop and deploy reproducible, maintainable machine-learning software, skills that matter just as much in research. The participants came from different business departments, with different cultures, missions, and business and engineering skills. The COVID-19 pandemic also required a swift move online, so I designed the course to be taught both on site and online.

Two challenges were new to me: keeping students motivated online, and fitting the course into the limited time that full-time professionals could spare. I planned a range of materials, including slides, shared code repositories, open-source software and a website with documentation and troubleshooting guides, and set up an issue tracker so that the teaching assistants I coordinated could give prompt, continuous support. Days before each session, students received the slides, the questions and coding exercises built into the software they would deploy, so they could prepare and review with little time away from their work.

These students already had strong collaborative skills, so I ran discussions that drew on their expertise, where their varied backgrounds exposed everyone to new ways of framing and solving problems. Course ratings were consistently above 9/10.

Mentoring

Mentoring calls for one-to-one exchanges, empathy, careful listening and a genuine interest in each person's experience and goals. I began practising these skills as a first-year university student, tutoring high-school students in biology. I later mentored several master's students in neuroscience and mathematics during my PhD and my postdoc at Stanford. As a lead data scientist, I oversaw the supervision and mentoring of junior and senior data scientists to help them mature professionally. It is rewarding to know that one of my former Scientific Methodology students is now a postdoctoral researcher in palaeoclimate reconstruction, and that one of my senior data scientists now supervises his own team.
