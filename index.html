Steeve Laquitaine, PhD

Research Scientist · Neural representations, information theory and inference

I develop mathematical and computational methods to understand what neural networks represent and how they use uncertain information.

My research connects information theory, generative modelling and computational neuroscience. I measure the information encoded by neural populations, distinguish competing mechanisms of inference, and identify biases that distort our understanding of neural computation. Across these problems, I combine theory, controlled experiments and reproducible software.

I am a research scientist at the Paris Vision Institute (Sorbonne Université, INSERM, CNRS) and an affiliate at Stanford University. Previously, I worked at EPFL's Blue Brain Project, Stanford University and the RIKEN Brain Science Institute, and as a Lead Data Scientist at Capgemini Engineering, where I supervised teams developing and deploying machine-learning software in production.

How I work

I look for cases where a convincing measurement supports the wrong explanation, then build the method and the experiment that tell the explanations apart.

More informative evaluations

Aggregate scores can hide the mechanism. Human estimates that matched Bayesian averages came from a strategy that switches between prior and evidence. The selection biases of spike sorters, not the neurons they miss, nearly halve the stimulus information of a recorded population.

Principled methods that scale

I derive measures from explicit axioms and first principles, then make them practical. Our decomposition of mutual information and our multi-scale information geometry are estimated with diffusion models, for large populations and natural images.

Experiments and software built for reuse

I design controlled experiments and ground-truth simulations, and release the methods as tested Python packages and public datasets. In industry, I supervised four teams developing and deploying machine-learning software in production.

Measuring what neural networks encode

An axiomatic decomposition of mutual information and a multi-scale information geometry, both estimated with diffusion models, show which stimuli and features a population encodes.

NeurIPS 2025 · NeurIPS 2026 oral

Telling apart mechanisms behind near-optimal behaviour

Human estimates matched Bayesian predictions on average, yet their full distributions revealed a strategy that switches between prior knowledge and sensory evidence.

Neuron 2018

Evaluating spike sorting against ground truth

A detailed cortical simulation with known spikes shows that sorters isolate about 10% of nearby neurons, and that their biases nearly halve stimulus information.

Research directions

I aim to extend this work toward understanding how learned representations support inference under changing conditions: when systems combine prior knowledge with new evidence, when they rely on shortcuts, and which experiments distinguish these strategies.

I am particularly interested in connecting principled measures of representation with behavioural tests that expose failures hidden by aggregate accuracy.
